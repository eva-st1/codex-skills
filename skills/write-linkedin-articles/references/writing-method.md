# Article writing method

## Voice discovery

Look for repeated choices across representative samples: audience and purpose, register, sentence rhythm, questions and fragments, humor, metaphor, certainty, emotional openness, openings, and endings. Record only traits supported by evidence, with confidence. A formal application should not automatically define the voice of a personal reflection.

Use a voice card to select among accurate phrasings. Do not manufacture opinions or anecdotes to make prose feel personal. When voice evidence is thin, state a provisional direction rather than describing the draft as an exact match.

## Angle selection

Ask:

- What problem or curiosity does the reader bring?
- What can this author add through experience, analysis, or synthesis?
- Is the central claim specific enough to test, disagree with, or apply?
- What can the reader do or understand afterward?

Useful angles include a consequential decision, repeated mistake, tension in research, before-and-after lesson, or honest field note. A narrow, defensible point is usually more useful than a broad trend recap. An opinion is legitimate when framed as the author's view and supported proportionately.

## AIDA without a template

- **Attention:** Make a truthful promise or reveal a concrete tension. The title and opening must agree; do not conceal essential context merely to force a click.
- **Interest:** Name the stakes and explain relevance. Use research to clarify the question, not decorate it with statistics.
- **Desire:** Show a better decision, capability, or understanding through a mechanism, example, or tradeoff. This need not be a sales pitch.
- **Action:** Offer a proportionate practice, reflection, experiment, or invitation. Avoid coercive engagement prompts and automatic “thoughts?” endings.

AIDA is a planning heuristic, not a performance prediction. Choose an actual structure that serves the idea:

| Structure | Useful progression |
| --- | --- |
| Argument | Observation → assumption → evidence → revised view → implications |
| Field note | Situation → surprise → interpretation → transferable lesson |
| How-to | Costly problem → principles → steps → limits → first action |
| Case study | Context → contribution → decision → result → limitations |
| Analysis | Signal → evidence → alternative explanations → judgment → what to watch |

## Revision questions

Review each dimension with concrete findings rather than a numerical quality score:

- **Truth:** Is every factual claim supported, attributed, or marked unresolved? Are team and personal contributions distinct?
- **Perspective:** Is there a defensible insight beyond a generic recap?
- **Usefulness:** Does the reader gain a decision, method, example, or worthwhile question?
- **Flow:** Does the piece fulfill its opening promise without withholding context?
- **Voice:** Does the language fit observed samples and explicit preferences?
- **Specificity:** Can abstract advice be replaced with a concrete mechanism or example?
- **Restraint:** Are certainty, emotional intimacy, superlatives, and calls to action earned?
- **Readability:** Do title, paragraphs, transitions, and subheads support the argument?

A narrow edit needs only the relevant checks; do not turn proofreading into a mandatory strategy workshop.

## Interviews

Ask only questions that could materially improve the article, in small rounds. Useful prompts:

- Which writing sample feels most like you, and what should this never sound like?
- Who needs this idea, and what should change for them?
- What did you personally observe or decide?
- Which details are public, and what remains confidential?
- What evidence supports the result or lesson?

Ask for clarification of consequential ambiguity. The user's clear answer is evidence; a separate confirmation ceremony is unnecessary.
